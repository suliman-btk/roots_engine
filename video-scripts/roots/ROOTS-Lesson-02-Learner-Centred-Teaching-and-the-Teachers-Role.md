## Scene 001

[natural pace] [warm] [welcoming]

السلام عليكم. Welcome back to ROOTS. In the first lesson, we asked how a teacher knows that learning has truly happened. At NAMA Foundation, we now look more closely at the shift that makes this question central: the move from concentrating mainly on content delivery toward learner-centred teaching. [short pause] By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain that shift and identify the decisions a teacher makes when learners, their experience, their participation, and their learning outcomes become the focus. Begin by thinking about where your attention usually goes while you teach.

## Scene 002

[natural pace] [reflective] [conversational]

When teaching is centred mainly on the teacher or the content, success can appear to mean completing the explanation, covering the planned pages, or reaching the end of the presentation. Those actions may be necessary, but they do not give the complete picture. [short pause] In ROOTS, we also ask what happened for the learner. Did the learner connect the idea with previous knowledge? Did the learner participate, question, practise, explain, or demonstrate understanding? This change in attention helps us examine teaching through learning rather than through delivery alone.

## Scene 003

[natural pace] [clear] [teacher-focused]

Learner-centred teaching means focusing on learners’ needs, ideas, learning preferences, participation, and outcomes. It recognizes that learners do not enter an experience as empty containers. They bring previous knowledge, feelings, abilities, questions, and life experience. [short pause] At NAMA Foundation, we use ROOTS to help teachers notice these differences and consider them when making teaching decisions. The purpose is not to prepare a completely different lesson for every person. It is to remain aware of individual learners and respond deliberately whenever the learning situation allows.

## Scene 004

[natural pace] [thoughtful] [steady]

The learner-centred shift changes the questions used during planning. Instead of asking only, “What will I teach?” we also ask, “What should learners know, do, or be willing to demonstrate?” We consider what they already know, how they may participate, and what evidence will reveal their progress. [short pause] The content remains important, but it now serves the intended learning. Activities, questions, explanations, practice, and review are selected because they support an outcome. This keeps the lesson directed toward what learners will carry away, not simply what the teacher will cover.

## Scene 005

[natural pace] [engaged] [encouraging]

Participation is another important part of learner-centred teaching. The ROOTS knowledge material describes learners solving problems, answering questions, developing questions of their own, discussing ideas, and explaining their thinking. These actions make learning visible and give learners an active place in the experience. [short pause] However, participation is not measured only by how much learners speak or move. A teacher considers whether the participation supports the intended outcome, involves meaningful thinking, and provides useful evidence about what learners understand and what support they may still need.

## Scene 006

[natural pace] [reassuring] [clear]

Learner-centred teaching does not remove the teacher from the learning process. It makes the teacher’s role broader and more intentional. The teacher still brings subject knowledge, prepares content, explains ideas, and provides direction. At the same time, the teacher designs the experience, listens to learners, observes their responses, asks purposeful questions, supports practice, and guides reflection. [short pause] In ROOTS, we see the teacher as someone who creates conditions for learning and then remains attentive to the evidence learners provide throughout the experience.

## Scene 007

[natural pace] [practical] [focused]

Observation helps a teacher turn learner-centred intentions into decisions. A learner’s answer, question, demonstration, written response, or difficulty can show where learning currently stands. The teacher can use that evidence to decide whether to continue, clarify, ask another question, provide feedback, or create another opportunity for practice. [short pause] This means the lesson is not treated as a fixed performance delivered in exactly the same way regardless of learner response. The teacher keeps the outcome in view while using evidence to guide the next appropriate teaching action.

## Scene 008

[natural pace] [thoughtful] [teacher-focused]

ROOTS also asks us to focus on each learner rather than seeing only the class as one group. The Focusing on Learner domain considers the learner’s curve of learning, previous knowledge, feelings, and experience. Its principle of individualization encourages teachers to make the experience distinct and personal whenever possible. [short pause] Individualization begins with awareness. Who needs further explanation? Who is ready to extend an idea? Who has experience that can support new learning? These questions help a teacher respond to differences without losing sight of the shared learning outcome.

## Scene 009

[natural pace] [warm] [encouraging]

Trusting the learner is equally important. The ROOTS material explains that adult learners bring knowledge and life experience to new learning. They benefit from opportunities to use that foundation and to move toward greater responsibility and self-direction. [short pause] Trust does not mean leaving learners without guidance. It means giving up some unnecessary control and making room for learners’ experience, abilities, questions, and choices. A teacher can still set a clear outcome and provide structure while recognizing that learners are active participants who contribute to the direction and meaning of their learning.

## Scene 010

[natural pace] [explanatory] [steady]

Learner-centred teaching also pays attention to interaction. Learners influence one another, and each group develops its own experience through those interactions. ROOTS therefore considers the positive spirit of the learning environment, learner motivation, attention, and the social dimension of learning. [short pause] The teacher’s responsibility is not simply to place learners together. It is to consider whether interaction helps them participate, exchange understanding, remain focused, and move toward the outcome. The quality and purpose of interaction matter more than interaction being present for its own sake.

## Scene 011

[natural pace] [practical] [reflective]

Let us bring these ideas together as a sequence of teacher decisions. First, clarify what learners should achieve. Next, consider what they already bring and how they can participate meaningfully. During the experience, observe their behaviour and gather evidence through questions, responses, practice, and reflection. Then use that evidence to choose the next action. [short pause] This sequence is not a separate method added to teaching. It is a learner-centred way of planning, facilitating, and reviewing teaching. It keeps the teacher responsive while the intended learning remains clear.

## Scene 012

[natural pace] [inviting] [conversational]

Now apply the shift to one lesson you know well. Identify the learning outcome first. Then ask three questions: what did the learners do, what evidence did I notice, and what decision did I make because of that evidence? [short pause] If your reflection focuses mainly on what you explained, look again from the learners’ side. Notice their questions, practice, connections, demonstrations, and points of difficulty. This reflection is not about judging the lesson as simply good or bad. It is about seeing where learner-centred decisions were already present and where they could become more deliberate.

## Scene 013

[slow pace] [warm] [confident]

Learner-centred teaching shifts attention from delivery alone to the complete learning experience. It begins with learners’ needs, ideas, prior experience, participation, and intended outcomes. The teacher remains essential as a designer, guide, observer, questioner, and facilitator who uses evidence to support the next decision. [short pause] In ROOTS, this shift prepares the ground for Active Deep Learner eXperience. In the next lesson, we will examine what makes learning active, what makes it deep, and how these qualities come together in ADLX without reducing learning to activity for its own sake.
